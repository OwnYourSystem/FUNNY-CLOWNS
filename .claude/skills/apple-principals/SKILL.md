# Apple Principals

A decision framework distilled from Apple's product and business model. Use it to design, evaluate, or critique any product (especially mobile apps and subscriptions) for premium positioning and retention.

## The core loop

Effortless product → trust → habit → accumulated personal value → high cost of leaving → willingness to pay premium → margins that fund keeping it effortless.

Every principle below feeds this loop. If a proposed feature or decision doesn't strengthen the loop, question it.

## The 7 principles

### 1. Hide complexity, show simplicity
The product can be sophisticated under the hood, but the user should never feel it. One clear main screen, one obvious action, zero manual. If a user needs onboarding slides to understand the product, the design failed.

**Test:** could a first-time user reach the core value with no instructions?

### 2. Ready to go, instantly useful
The iPhone works out of the box. Deliver value in the first session, before any setup, before any account. Let people feel the payoff first, ask for commitment second. Front-loading registration and configuration loses users before they see value.

**Test:** what does the user get in the first 60 seconds, logged out?

### 3. Opinionated defaults instead of options
Apple decides for you: one button, one port, one way. Don't give users 40 settings, give them the right answer. People pay for judgment, not a toolbox. "Do this 10-minute session now" beats a library of 5,000 sessions with no guidance.

**Test:** for each setting, ask "could we decide this for the user instead?"

### 4. Define expectations, don't chase features
Apple ships features late and nobody cares, because users trust its judgment. A product doesn't need everything competitors have. It needs users to believe what's there is deliberate. Cutting features is a premium signal; feature bloat reads as cheap.

**Test:** does this feature exist because users need it, or because a competitor has it?

### 5. Build switching cost through accumulated value
The subscription engine. The longer someone uses the product, the more it holds: history, streaks, data, tuned recommendations, saved work. Canceling must mean losing something personal, not just losing access to features.

Three moats, best products hold two:
- **Data** (Strava, Whoop, Notion): history and records that exist nowhere else
- **Habit** (Duolingo, Headspace): streaks, routines, identity
- **Infrastructure** (1Password, YNAB): the product runs part of the user's life

**Test (the morning-after test):** if the user cancels tonight, what do they lose tomorrow morning? "Nothing much" = no business. "My whole system" = a business.

### 6. Reliability as identity
It just works, every time. One sync failure or lost entry burns more trust than 10 new features rebuild. Premium price demands premium reliability; the moment the product feels fragile, the price feels like a rip-off.

**Test:** what's the worst data-loss or failure scenario, and is it impossible?

### 7. Charge for the whole, not the parts
Apple doesn't itemize. Don't scatter 12 paywalled unlocks through the product. Sell one clean promise: "this handles X for you completely." One price, one or two tiers, no nickel-and-diming. Complexity in pricing signals insecurity in the product.

**Test:** can the pricing be explained in one sentence?

## The business truth behind it

Apple doesn't sell the best phone. It sells the phone that's hardest to leave, then charges premium margins because you won't. Specs are a conversation Apple opted out of; it competes on retention. Competitors (Samsung, Xiaomi) must win the spec war every year because they have weak lock-in. Apple's moat is switching cost built on a product good enough that staying feels like protecting something, not being held hostage.

The lock-in only works because the product genuinely delivers. Nobody stays in a bad ecosystem; they leave angry.

## Reference examples

| App | Moat | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Duolingo | Habit | Streak that exists nowhere else; tells you exactly what to do next |
| Strava | Data | Years of training history, PRs, social graph |
| Spotify | Data | Playlists and a taste algorithm trained on you |
| Notion | Data + Infrastructure | Your entire second brain lives inside it |
| Headspace | Habit | Opinionated "do this now" defaults; pay for judgment |
| Whoop / Oura | Data | Health baselines that compound daily |
| 1Password | Infrastructure | Holds everything, works invisibly, migration unthinkable |
| YNAB | Infrastructure + Habit | Canceling feels like losing control of your money |

**Churn counter-examples:** news apps, scanners, VPNs, photo editors. Nothing accumulates; day 1 equals day 500; canceling costs nothing.

## How to apply this skill

When reviewing or designing a product:

1. Run the morning-after test first (principle 5). It's the kill question.
2. Walk the other 6 principles as a checklist, using each test question.
3. For every gap found, name the problem, give the fix, show a concrete better version.
4. End with a verdict: does the core loop close, or where does it break?

Be direct. Score against the principles honestly; don't soften a broken loop into "some opportunities for improvement." A product that fails the morning-after test fails, full stop.
